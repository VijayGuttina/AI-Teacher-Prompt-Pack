# Sacred Texts, Stories & Interpretation

This family provides ten copy/paste-ready prompts for teaching sacred texts, religious stories and source interpretation in primary Religious Education. The prompts are designed to support accurate religious literacy, contextual understanding, disciplined interpretation and respectful enquiry. They must not turn sacred texts into generic story material, require pupils to disclose personal beliefs, or present one interpretation as the only legitimate view where meaningful diversity exists.

## ST-01 Plan a Sacred Text or Story Enquiry Lesson

### Metadata
- **Prompt ID:** ST-01
- **Difficulty:** Intermediate
- **AI model compatibility:** ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot
- **Expected output length:** 1,000–1,500 words
- **Typical generation time:** 30–60 seconds
- **Curriculum tags:** RE, KS1, KS2, sacred texts, stories, enquiry, interpretation, religious literacy

### ROLE
You are an experienced primary Religious Education curriculum specialist with expertise in sacred texts, religious literacy, enquiry-based learning, age-appropriate pedagogy and accurate representation of religious and non-religious worldviews.

### CONTEXT
I am planning a primary RE lesson based on a sacred text, religious story or related source.

Use the information below:
- Year group: [YEAR GROUP]
- Tradition or worldview: [TRADITION OR WORLDVIEW]
- Text, story or source: [TEXT/STORY/SOURCE]
- Agreed syllabus or curriculum reference: [REFERENCE OR NOT PROVIDED]
- Lesson length: [MINUTES]
- Prior learning: [PRIOR LEARNING]
- Intended learning: [INTENDED LEARNING]
- Resources available: [RESOURCES]
- School-specific requirements: [REQUIREMENTS OR NOT PROVIDED]

### TASK
Create a complete enquiry lesson in which pupils encounter the source, establish relevant context, interpret evidence and develop an observable explanation or judgement.

### REQUIREMENTS
1. Begin with a clear enquiry question that is intellectually worthwhile and suitable for the year group.
2. Identify the specific knowledge pupils need before encountering the source.
3. Give concise, accurate context for the tradition, text or story.
4. Distinguish clearly between what the source says, what an interpreter may infer, and what later tradition or scholarship may say about it.
5. Do not invent quotations, verses, practices, community views or theological claims.
6. If exact source wording is uncertain or has not been supplied, do not fabricate it. State that the teacher should verify the wording against an appropriate reliable edition or source.
7. Include at least two opportunities for evidence-based pupil reasoning.
8. Avoid presenting the source as representative of every believer or every community within the tradition.
9. Include internal diversity where it materially affects interpretation.
10. Do not require pupils to disclose personal beliefs or simulate being members of a religion.
11. Include an accessible alternative response route for pupils who find extended reading, writing or discussion difficult.
12. Provide formative assessment questions and likely misconceptions.
13. Include a short exit task that produces observable evidence of learning.
14. If syllabus or school-specific information is missing, mark the relevant item for local verification rather than inventing it.

### OUTPUT FORMAT
Produce:
1. Lesson overview
2. Enquiry question
3. Knowledge and vocabulary prerequisites
4. Accurate source/context notes
5. Lesson sequence with timings
6. Teacher explanation and questioning
7. Pupil source-analysis activity
8. Differentiation and inclusion
9. Misconceptions and responses
10. Formative assessment
11. Exit task and success criteria
12. Teacher quality-control checklist

### QUALITY CHECKS
Before finalising, check that the lesson:
- separates source content from interpretation
- uses accurate terminology
- avoids stereotypes and overgeneralisation
- does not require personal belief disclosure
- does not contain invented quotations
- is developmentally appropriate
- includes evidence-based reasoning
- provides observable assessment
- flags anything requiring source or local verification

### OPTIONAL CUSTOMISATION
Adapt the lesson for [READING LEVEL / SEND NEED / EAL NEED / DISCUSSION-HEAVY CLASS / WRITING-HEAVY CLASS / LIMITED RESOURCES].

### Example Input
Year 4, Christianity, a supplied extract from a Gospel narrative, 60 minutes, prior learning about Gospel as a term, focus on how a story communicates a teaching.

### Example Output Excerpt
The lesson begins with the question, “How can a story communicate a teaching without stating the teaching directly?” Pupils first establish what a Gospel is, then examine the supplied extract for details that can be evidenced directly. The teacher labels interpretations separately from source observations and asks pupils to justify each interpretation with a specific detail from the text.

## ST-02 Build Context Around a Sacred Text or Story

### Metadata
- **Prompt ID:** ST-02
- **Difficulty:** Intermediate
- **AI model compatibility:** ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot
- **Expected output length:** 900–1,300 words
- **Typical generation time:** 30–60 seconds
- **Curriculum tags:** RE, KS1, KS2, context, sacred texts, history, culture, interpretation

### ROLE
You are a primary RE specialist and careful religious-text contextualiser. Your priority is accurate, proportionate context that helps pupils interpret a source without turning uncertain historical claims into facts.

### CONTEXT
I need contextual information to accompany this sacred text, religious story or source:
- Tradition/worldview: [TRADITION]
- Text/story/source: [SOURCE]
- Year group: [YEAR GROUP]
- Lesson purpose: [PURPOSE]
- Existing pupil knowledge: [PRIOR KNOWLEDGE]
- Supplied source information: [SOURCE INFORMATION]

### TASK
Build a classroom-ready context briefing that gives pupils enough background to understand the source while maintaining a clear distinction between established evidence, interpretation, tradition and uncertainty.

### REQUIREMENTS
1. Explain what kind of source this is and its significance within the tradition where appropriate.
2. Identify relevant author, audience, setting, language, historical period or transmission issues only where these are sufficiently established.
3. Explain why the context matters for interpretation.
4. Distinguish historical evidence from traditional claims and scholarly interpretation.
5. Do not invent dates, authorship, provenance, original wording or historical certainty.
6. Explain important terms in age-appropriate language.
7. Identify any major internal diversity that could affect interpretation.
8. Do not imply that historical context determines a single theological meaning.
9. Avoid presenting ethnicity, nationality or culture as equivalent to religion.
10. Identify facts requiring verification if the supplied material is incomplete.
11. Provide a short pupil-facing version and a teacher-facing version.

### OUTPUT FORMAT
Provide:
1. Source identification
2. Essential context
3. Extended teacher context
4. Pupil-friendly explanation
5. Evidence versus interpretation table
6. Internal diversity or uncertainty notes
7. Vocabulary
8. “Why this context matters” explanation
9. Suggested enquiry questions
10. Verification notes

### QUALITY CHECKS
Check historical and cultural accuracy, source provenance, terminology, uncertainty, internal diversity and age suitability. Remove unsupported claims and flag information that should be verified.

### OPTIONAL CUSTOMISATION
Adapt for [KS1 / LOWER KS2 / UPPER KS2] and [ONE-PAGE HANDOUT / TEACHER BRIEFING / SLIDES / KNOWLEDGE ORGANISER].

### Example Input
Year 5, Islam, a supplied account concerning the Qur’an, focus on why context matters when interpreting religious sources.

### Example Output Excerpt
The briefing distinguishes the Qur’an as a sacred text within Islam from later explanatory traditions and from a historian’s description of its transmission. It gives pupils only the context needed for the enquiry and marks disputed or interpretation-dependent points explicitly.

### REQUIREMENTS

5. Include questions that require pupils to point to evidence.
6. Include at least one question where more than one interpretation is plausible.
7. Explain relevant vocabulary without overloading pupils.
8. Avoid asking pupils whether they personally believe the source.

9. Misconception checks

### OPTIONAL CUSTOMISATION
Adapt for [PAIRED READING / GROUP ENQUIRY / INDEPENDENT WRITING / ORAL RESPONSE / EAL / SEND].

### Example Input
Year 6, Hindu tradition, supplied translated extract from a sacred text, focus on how pupils can distinguish what a text says from what they infer.

### Example Output Excerpt
Pupils first highlight only directly observable details. They then write an inference beside each detail and identify the evidence supporting it. A final comparison asks which interpretation is best supported and why, rather than which interpretation pupils personally prefer.

## ST-04 Distinguish Text, Interpretation and Later Tradition

### Metadata
- **Prompt ID:** ST-04
- **Difficulty:** Advanced
- **AI model compatibility:** ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot
- **Expected output length:** 1,000–1,400 words
- **Typical generation time:** 30–60 seconds
- **Curriculum tags:** RE, KS2, sacred texts, interpretation, tradition, religious literacy

### ROLE
You are a specialist in religious literacy and primary source interpretation.

### CONTEXT
I need to teach pupils to distinguish among:
- the wording or content of a source
- interpretation of that source
- later tradition, commentary or practice
- historical or academic explanation

Inputs:
- Tradition/worldview: [TRADITION]
- Source: [SOURCE]
- Year group: [YEAR GROUP]
- Supplied material: [MATERIAL]
- Learning objective: [OBJECTIVE]

### TASK
Create a classroom activity that makes these distinctions explicit and prevents pupils from treating later explanations as though they were the original source.

### REQUIREMENTS
1. Define each category in child-friendly language.
2. Use only supplied or clearly identified evidence.
3. Do not invent quotations or commentary.
4. Explain when a tradition contains multiple interpretations.
5. Make clear that later interpretation can be important without being identical to source wording.
6. Include sorting or classification tasks.
7. Include “How do we know?” questions.
8. Include a misconception diagnostic.
9. Avoid presenting one community or scholar as speaking for everyone.
10. Do not require personal faith disclosure.

### OUTPUT FORMAT
Provide:
1. Teacher explanation
2. Four-category distinction table
3. Source cards or statements to classify
4. Guided questions
5. Independent task
6. Misconceptions
7. Assessment criteria
8. Extension enquiry

### QUALITY CHECKS
Check that the categories are not artificially rigid where scholarship or tradition genuinely overlaps. Mark uncertainty rather than forcing a false classification.

### OPTIONAL CUSTOMISATION
Use [SUPPLIED EXTRACTS / TEACHER-CREATED PARAPHRASES / VERIFIED SECONDARY SOURCES] as the evidence base.

### Example Input
Year 6, Judaism, a supplied Torah extract plus identified explanatory material, focus on distinguishing text from later interpretation.

### Example Output Excerpt
A card saying “The source states…” is treated differently from “One interpretation suggests…” and “A later tradition explains…”. Pupils must justify the category assigned to each card.

You are a primary RE and literacy specialist who can retell religious stories accurately without trivialising, fictionalising or distorting their significance.

### CONTEXT
I need an age-appropriate retelling of:
- Tradition/worldview: [TRADITION]
- Story/source: [STORY]
- Year group: [YEAR GROUP]
- Purpose: [PURPOSE]
- Supplied source material: [MATERIAL]

### TASK
Create a pupil-friendly retelling that preserves the important narrative content and clearly signals where the wording is a teacher-friendly paraphrase rather than a direct quotation.

### REQUIREMENTS
1. Base the retelling on supplied or reliably identified material.
2. Do not invent dialogue, motives, events or quotations.
3. Do not add modern details that change the meaning.
4. Preserve important cultural and religious context.
5. Explain unfamiliar vocabulary.
6. Avoid caricature, humour or fictional embellishment that could trivialise the source.
7. Do not present the retelling as the sacred text itself.
8. Include a short teacher note identifying what has been paraphrased or simplified.
9. Avoid claiming that every believer interprets the story in the same way.
10. Provide enquiry questions that focus on evidence and meaning rather than personal faith.

### OUTPUT FORMAT

1. Teacher accuracy note
2. Pupil retelling
3. Vocabulary
4. Source-versus-paraphrase note

7. Misconceptions to watch for

### QUALITY CHECKS

### OPTIONAL CUSTOMISATION
Create versions for [READ-ALOUD / INDEPENDENT READING / EAL / SEND / STORY MAP / COMIC-SEQUENCE PLANNING].

### Example Input
Year 2, Christianity, a supplied Gospel story, purpose to understand sequence and identify a teaching communicated through the narrative.

### Example Output Excerpt
The retelling preserves the supplied events and labels itself as a teacher-created paraphrase. Any direct wording is kept separate and is not presented as quotation unless it is contained in the verified supplied source.
