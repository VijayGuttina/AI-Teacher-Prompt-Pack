# Safety, Risk & Digital Citizenship

This family provides ten copy/paste-ready ChatGPT workflows for teaching primary pupils about personal safety, risk, privacy, online behaviour, digital citizenship and evaluating information. Prompts are designed to support education rather than surveillance, counselling, diagnosis or individual safeguarding intervention.

## Family-wide controls

Every workflow must:
- use developmentally appropriate language, examples and expectations;
- distinguish teaching about safety from responding to a live safeguarding concern;
- avoid asking pupils to disclose passwords, private messages, identifying information, family circumstances or other sensitive personal data;
- never invent school safeguarding, reporting, acceptable-use or digital-device procedures;
- use supplied school policies where available and explicitly flag missing local requirements;
- avoid victim-blaming and avoid implying that a child is responsible for an adult's unsafe behaviour;
- distinguish ordinary mistakes, disagreement, risk and unsafe behaviour rather than treating them as interchangeable;
- teach practical help-seeking and escalation without presenting ChatGPT as a safeguarding reporting route;
- avoid fear-based teaching, shame, humiliation and exaggerated claims about online threats;
- use current reliable evidence where laws, platforms, regulations, health claims, cyber threats or rapidly changing technologies are involved;
- distinguish evidence, opinion, inference, advertising, persuasion and misinformation;
- protect privacy when generating examples and use fictionalised scenarios unless the teacher explicitly supplies appropriate non-sensitive material;
- assess observable knowledge, reasoning and safe-choice skills rather than pupils' private experiences, family practices or willingness to disclose;
- preserve the intended learning construct when adapting materials for SEND, EAL or other access needs.

## SD-01 Plan a Digital and Personal Safety Lesson

**Difficulty:** Beginner  
**AI model compatibility:** ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot  
**Expected output length:** 900 to 1,400 words  
**Typical generation time:** 30 to 60 seconds  
**Curriculum tags:** PSHE, KS1, KS2, Safety, Personal Safety, Digital Safety, Risk, Help-Seeking

### ROLE

You are an experienced UK primary PSHE curriculum designer and safeguarding-aware classroom practitioner. You design lessons that teach practical safety knowledge and decision-making without creating fear or requiring children to disclose private experiences.

### CONTEXT

I am planning a primary PSHE lesson on digital and/or personal safety.

Year group: [YEAR GROUP]
Lesson length: [MINUTES]
Topic/focus: [SAFETY FOCUS]
Prior learning: [PRIOR LEARNING]
School or trust guidance that must be followed: [POLICY OR GUIDANCE, IF AVAILABLE]
Pupil access considerations: [SEND/EAL/OTHER NEEDS]
Resources available: [RESOURCES]

If any school-specific safeguarding, reporting, device-use or acceptable-use procedure is missing, do not invent it. State what the teacher needs to confirm locally.

### TASK

Create a complete, classroom-ready lesson that teaches the specified safety concept through explicit teaching, guided practice and age-appropriate scenarios.

### REQUIREMENTS

- Define the precise knowledge pupils should learn.
- Separate hazards, risk, protective behaviour and help-seeking.
- Include realistic examples without sensationalising harm.
- Use fictional scenarios rather than asking pupils to describe real incidents.
- Include at least one decision-making routine pupils can reuse.
- Explain when a child should seek help from a trusted adult, using locally adaptable wording.
- Include misconceptions and corrections.
- Include adaptations that preserve the intended safety construct.
- Do not ask pupils to reveal passwords, private conversations, personal addresses, family circumstances or safeguarding experiences.
- Include a short teacher safeguarding note explaining what to do if a pupil makes a disclosure, but do not invent a school procedure. Tell the teacher to follow their actual safeguarding policy.

### OUTPUT FORMAT

Provide:
1. Lesson title
2. Learning intention
3. Success criteria
4. Key vocabulary
5. Prior knowledge check
6. Teacher explanation and modelling
7. Guided scenario activity
8. Independent or paired task
9. Help-seeking routine
10. Misconceptions and corrections
11. Inclusion adaptations
12. Assessment and exit ticket
13. Resources
14. Teacher safeguarding and policy note
15. Optional extension

### QUALITY CHECKS

Before finalising, verify that the lesson is developmentally appropriate, practical, non-alarmist, privacy-aware, safeguarding-conscious and assessable. Confirm that no invented school procedures or individual medical/safeguarding advice has been included.

### OPTIONAL CUSTOMISATION

If useful, also produce a [KS1/LOWER KS2/UPPER KS2] version and a [SHORTER/LONGER] version without changing the core learning objective.

### Example Input

Year group: Year 5  
Lesson length: 45 minutes  
Topic/focus: recognising unsafe online contact and seeking help

### Example Output

A structured lesson in which pupils learn to recognise warning signs, pause before responding, avoid sharing personal information and seek help from an appropriate trusted adult, with fictional scenarios and an explicit safeguarding boundary.

**Typical generation time:** 45 to 90 seconds

### ROLE

You are a primary PSHE and critical-digital-literacy specialist. You teach pupils to evaluate information without encouraging them to diagnose themselves or replace professional advice with internet searches.

### CONTEXT

I need a lesson on evaluating online information or health-related claims.

Year group: [YEAR GROUP]
Lesson length: [MINUTES]
Topic: [CLAIM-TYPE]
Reliable sources supplied by school: [SOURCES, IF AVAILABLE]
Prior learning: [PRIOR LEARNING]

### TASK

Create a classroom-ready lesson teaching pupils how to assess whether an online claim is trustworthy.

### REQUIREMENTS

Teach an age-appropriate evaluation framework covering:
- who produced the information;
- purpose and audience;
- evidence offered;
- whether the claim can be independently checked;

- what to do when evidence is insufficient.

For health claims, explicitly distinguish health education from diagnosis or medical treatment. Do not ask pupils to assess their own symptoms or make medical decisions.

Use fictional or teacher-supplied examples. Do not fabricate statistics, studies, quotations or health guidance. If a current claim requires verification, identify reliable current sources the teacher should consult rather than inventing details.

### OUTPUT FORMAT

Provide:
1. Lesson overview
2. Learning intention
3. Success criteria
4. Evaluation framework
5. Teacher modelling
6. Three fictional examples
7. Guided analysis questions
8. Independent task
9. Misconceptions
10. Inclusion adaptations
11. Assessment rubric
12. Current-information verification note
13. Teacher health/safeguarding boundary note

### QUALITY CHECKS

Check that the lesson teaches source evaluation rather than medical diagnosis. Check that examples distinguish evidence from assertion and that no current health facts have been invented.

### OPTIONAL CUSTOMISATION

### Example Input

## SD-08 Diagnose Misconceptions About Digital and Personal Safety

**Difficulty:** Advanced

**Expected output length:** 1,100 to 1,700 words  
**Typical generation time:** 45 to 90 seconds  
**Curriculum tags:** PSHE, KS1, KS2, Assessment, Misconceptions, Digital Safety, Personal Safety

### ROLE

You are an expert primary PSHE assessment designer specialising in misconception diagnosis.

### CONTEXT

Analyse these anonymised pupil responses:

Year group: [YEAR GROUP]
Learning focus: [FOCUS]
Pupil responses: [RESPONSES]
Learning objective taught: [OBJECTIVE]

Do not infer personal experiences, family circumstances or safeguarding history from responses.

### TASK

Diagnose the underlying conceptual errors and design targeted teaching responses.

### REQUIREMENTS

For each response:
- identify what the pupil appears to understand;
- identify the specific misconception or reasoning error;
- distinguish factual error from incomplete reasoning;
- avoid interpreting confidence or wording as evidence of personal experience;
- provide a precise corrective explanation;
- provide a short follow-up task;
- provide a reassessment question;
- identify whether the issue is knowledge, vocabulary, risk reasoning, privacy understanding, source evaluation or help-seeking.

If a response potentially indicates a safeguarding disclosure, do not investigate it through the prompt. Flag it neutrally and instruct the teacher to follow the school's actual safeguarding procedure.

### OUTPUT FORMAT

Use a table with columns for response, secure understanding, misconception, likely cause, corrective teaching, practice task, reassessment and teacher note.

Then provide a five-minute whole-class reteach and a targeted small-group intervention.

### QUALITY CHECKS

Check that diagnosis concerns the taught construct rather than pupil personality, family background or personal experience. Check safeguarding boundaries.

### OPTIONAL CUSTOMISATION

Add [SEND/EAL] adaptations and [RETRIEVAL/SCENARIO/EXPLICIT TEACHING] intervention formats.

### Example Input

Year group: Year 4  
Learning focus: personal information and privacy  
Pupil response: “It is always safe to tell someone your school because they already know children go there.”

### Example Output

Diagnosis identifies an overgeneralisation about context and audience, followed by teaching that privacy depends on who is asking, why they are asking and what information is being combined.
