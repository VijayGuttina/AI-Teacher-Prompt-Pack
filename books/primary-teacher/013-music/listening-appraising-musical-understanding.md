# Listening, Appraising & Musical Understanding

This workflow family contains 10 copy/paste-ready ChatGPT workflows for developing purposeful listening, appraising and musical understanding in primary music.

The prompts are designed to move pupils beyond naming instruments or giving unsupported opinions. They support attentive listening, musical vocabulary, comparison, evidence-based explanation, contextual understanding and respectful discussion.

---

## LA-01 Guided Musical Listening

```yaml
id: LA-01
title: Guided Musical Listening
category: Listening, Appraising & Musical Understanding
tags: [listening, appraising, musical elements, KS1, KS2, music]
prompt_difficulty: Intermediate
ai_model_compatibility: [ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot]
expected_output_length: Medium
typical_generation_time: 1-2 minutes
```

### ROLE
You are an experienced primary music teacher and curriculum specialist. You design listening activities that develop attentive musical listening rather than passive exposure to music.

### CONTEXT
I am teaching [YEAR GROUP] pupils. I want them to listen attentively to [MUSICAL WORK OR TYPE OF MUSIC]. Their prior musical knowledge is [PRIOR KNOWLEDGE]. The lesson lasts [MINUTES].

### TASK
Create a guided listening activity that helps pupils notice, describe and discuss specific musical features.

### REQUIREMENTS
- Identify 3 to 5 musical features pupils should listen for, appropriate to their age.
- Begin with an open first listen before directing attention to specific features.
- Use accessible questions before specialist terminology where appropriate.
- Include repeated listening with a different focus each time.
- Require pupils to give musical evidence for observations.
- Include opportunities for movement, drawing, marking a pulse, verbal response or simple notation where useful.
- Distinguish observation from personal preference.
- Include teacher modelling of a strong listening response.
- Include likely misconceptions and corrective prompts.
- Provide an inclusive response route for pupils who find extended verbal explanation difficult.

### OUTPUT FORMAT
Provide:
1. Listening objective
2. Success criteria
3. Prior knowledge retrieval
4. First listen
5. Focused second listen
6. Focused third listen
7. Pupil discussion questions
8. Teacher modelling
9. Assessment opportunities
10. Misconceptions
11. Inclusion and adaptation
12. Plenary

### QUALITY CHECKS
Check that every question has a clear musical purpose, that pupils are asked to refer to what they hear, and that the activity does not depend on unsupported factual claims about the musical work.

### OPTIONAL CUSTOMISATION
Adapt the activity for [SPECIFIC WORK], [YEAR GROUP] and [LISTENING FOCUS].

### Example Input
Year 3, 30 minutes, listening to a contrasting orchestral movement, focus on dynamics, tempo and instrumentation.

### Example Output
Pupils listen once without interruption, then listen again while tracking changes in dynamics and tempo, followed by a third listen focused on instrumental timbres. They record observations and explain one claim using specific musical evidence.

### OPTIONAL CUSTOMISATION
Create printable vocabulary cards, sentence stems or a pupil glossary for [TERMS].

### Example Input
Year 4, vocabulary: ostinato, texture, dynamics, tempo and timbre.

### Example Output
Each term is introduced through a short listening or practical example. Pupils sort observations using the vocabulary, then describe a new musical extract using at least three target terms and specific evidence.

Design a comparative listening lesson that helps pupils identify similarities and differences and support their judgements with musical evidence.

- Identify a manageable number of comparison criteria.
- Include at least one first-listen response for each work before detailed comparison.
- Compare pulse, rhythm, dynamics, tempo, texture, timbre, structure or other relevant elements only where genuinely useful.
- Include a comparison grid or equivalent recording structure.
- Model the difference between a vague opinion and an evidence-based musical observation.
- Include paired or group discussion followed by independent response.

- Include misconceptions and vocabulary support.
- Provide an accessible response route for pupils who need reduced writing demands.

8. Independent comparison task
9. Assessment rubric
10. Misconceptions and interventions
11. Inclusion and extension
12. Plenary

### QUALITY CHECKS
Check that comparisons are based on audible musical features, that factual context is not presented as certain when it is not known, and that the two works are compared using equivalent criteria.

### OPTIONAL CUSTOMISATION
Adapt the comparison for two works from different periods, cultures, composers, genres or musical traditions while maintaining culturally respectful language.

### Example Input
Year 6 comparing two contrasting pieces with different textures and structures.

### Example Output
Pupils independently listen to both works, record observations against the same criteria, discuss similarities and differences, and write or record a supported comparison using at least three specific pieces of musical evidence.

### TASK
Create a practical listening lesson that helps pupils hear, identify, represent and explain musical structure.

### REQUIREMENTS
- Begin with the idea of contrasting or repeated sections before introducing formal labels where appropriate.
- Use a clear visual representation such as letters, symbols, colours or simple boxes.
- Include repeated listening with a specific structural focus.
- Ask pupils to identify what changed and what stayed the same.
- Link structural changes to musical evidence such as melody, rhythm, texture, dynamics, timbre or instrumentation where appropriate.

- Include misconceptions and vocabulary support.

11. Inclusion and extension
12. Plenary

### QUALITY CHECKS
Check that structural labels accurately reflect the selected example and that pupils are taught to hear structural relationships rather than simply memorise form names.

### OPTIONAL CUSTOMISATION
Create a structural listening map for [MUSICAL WORK] using age-appropriate notation.

### Example Input
Year 5, pupils know verse and chorus, learning to identify contrasting sections in an instrumental work.

### Example Output
Pupils listen for repeated and contrasting sections, assign simple A/B labels, create a visual listening map, then explain what musical features signal each new section.

### OPTIONAL CUSTOMISATION
Create a listening bank for [INSTRUMENT FAMILY] or [YEAR GROUP].

### Example Input
Year 2, pupils learning to distinguish woodwind, brass and percussion sounds through listening.

### Example Output
Pupils hear short examples without seeing the instruments, describe sound qualities, make an initial identification, then reveal and discuss the instruments and families using accurate vocabulary.

### TASK
Create a lesson that teaches pupils to form a personal musical response and support it with specific evidence from what they hear.

### REQUIREMENTS
- Explicitly distinguish "I like it" from an evidence-based musical response.
- Do not suggest there is one correct personal opinion about music.

- Require pupils to identify audible evidence before evaluating its effect.
- Model respectful disagreement.
- Include vocabulary appropriate to the pupils.
- Include an accessible spoken, drawn or recorded response route.
- Include an extension requiring pupils to explain how a musical decision contributes to effect.

7. Independent response task
8. Assessment criteria
9. Respectful discussion expectations
10. Inclusion and extension
11. Plenary

### QUALITY CHECKS
Check that pupils are not rewarded for a particular taste or opinion, that evidence is genuinely audible, and that evaluative language remains respectful.

### OPTIONAL CUSTOMISATION
Adapt the task for a specific genre, musical tradition, composer or performance context.

### Example Input
Year 6, pupils tend to give one-word opinions when appraising music.

### Example Output
Pupils first record a personal response, then identify two audible musical features that influenced it, before constructing a short explanation using evidence and respectful evaluative language.

### Example Input
Year 5, exploring a musical tradition through one specific work and its community context.

### Example Output

## LA-10 Diagnose Listening and Musical Understanding Difficulties

```yaml
id: LA-10
title: Diagnose Listening and Musical Understanding Difficulties
category: Listening, Appraising & Musical Understanding
tags: [diagnosis, assessment, listening, intervention, KS1, KS2]
prompt_difficulty: Advanced
ai_model_compatibility: [ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot]
expected_output_length: Long
typical_generation_time: 2-3 minutes
```

### ROLE
You are an experienced primary music teacher, assessment specialist and intervention planner. You analyse observable musical learning difficulties without making unsupported clinical or developmental diagnoses.

### CONTEXT
I have observed the following difficulty in a pupil: [OBSERVATION]. The pupil is in [YEAR GROUP]. They can currently [STRENGTHS]. The difficulty appears during [LISTENING/APPRAISING ACTIVITY]. Available intervention time is [TIME].

### TASK
Analyse the observed difficulty and create a short diagnostic and intervention plan for listening and musical understanding.

### REQUIREMENTS
- Separate observed behaviour from assumptions about its cause.
- Identify whether the difficulty may involve attention to sound, auditory discrimination within the task, musical vocabulary, memory, comparison, explanation, notation, language demands or another observable factor.
- Do not diagnose a medical, neurological, sensory or developmental condition from classroom evidence.
- Provide 2 to 4 diagnostic activities that vary one demand at a time.
- Explain what the teacher should observe in each activity.
- Recommend a short intervention sequence based on the evidence.
- Reduce language or writing demands where necessary to determine whether musical understanding is present.
- Include repeated listening and gradual removal of scaffolds.
- Define observable indicators of improvement.
- State when further school-based assessment or support should be considered rather than inferring a cause.

### OUTPUT FORMAT
Provide:
1. Observation summary
2. Evidence versus assumption
3. Possible musical learning explanations
4. Diagnostic activity 1
5. Diagnostic activity 2
6. Diagnostic activity 3, if needed
7. Interpretation guide
8. Intervention sequence
9. Inclusion and accessibility adjustments
10. Progress indicators
11. Review point
12. Teacher recording template

### QUALITY CHECKS
Check that no clinical diagnosis is made, that the diagnostic activities isolate relevant musical demands, and that interventions target the observed learning construct rather than a presumed underlying condition.

### OPTIONAL CUSTOMISATION
If I provide observations from several lessons, compare them and identify the strongest evidence for the next teaching priority.

### Example Input
Year 3 pupil can identify a loud or quiet section when given choices but struggles to explain the change verbally.

### Example Output
The plan tests whether the main barrier is musical recognition or verbal expression by using pointing, symbol selection and practical demonstration before requiring spoken explanation. Intervention then strengthens musical vocabulary and evidence-based explanation while preserving the listening construct.
