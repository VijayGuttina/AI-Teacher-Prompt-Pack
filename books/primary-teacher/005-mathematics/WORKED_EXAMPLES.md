# Primary Mathematics Worked Examples

These examples demonstrate the quality and intended use of the workflow library. They are category-level examples, not additional workflows.

## 1. Number and Place Value

**Workflow:** NM-05 — Number-line reasoning

**Teacher input**

> Year 3. Create a number-line reasoning activity from 0 to 1,000. Pupils should reason about intervals and missing positions rather than simply read labels. Include answers.

**Representative output**

**Setup:** Draw a number line from 0 to 1,000 divided into 10 equal intervals.

**Tasks:**
1. What number belongs halfway between 300 and 500?
2. A point is one interval after 600. What number is it?
3. A point is three intervals before 900. What number is it?
4. Point A is at 400 and Point B is at 750. Which is further from 500? Explain.

**Answers:** 400; 700; 600; B is 250 away, while A is 100 away.

**Why useful:** The workflow turns a number-line representation into magnitude and interval reasoning rather than a label-reading exercise.

**Teacher check/customisation:** Confirm that the physical number line uses exactly the stated scale and that pupils understand each interval represents 100.

## 2. Calculation and Fluency

**Workflow:** CF-02 — Calculation strategy comparison

**Teacher input**

> Year 5. Compare efficient mental strategies for 398 + 247. Explain the reasoning and when each strategy is useful.

**Representative output**

**Strategy 1: Compensation**
398 + 247 = 400 + 247 − 2 = 645.

**Strategy 2: Partition**
398 + 200 + 40 + 7 = 645.

**Strategy 3: Adjust both numbers**
398 + 247 = 400 + 245 = 645.

**Teacher prompt:** Which strategy involved the least mental adjustment for you? Why?

**Why useful:** The workflow exposes flexibility rather than presenting one procedure as universally best.

**Teacher check/customisation:** Select strategies that match what pupils have already been taught and avoid introducing an unfamiliar method merely because it is mathematically valid.

## 3. Fractions, Decimals and Percentages

**Workflow:** FD-02 — Fraction equivalence and comparison

**Teacher input**

> Year 5. Teach pupils to compare 3/4 and 5/8 using representations and reasoning rather than relying only on a formal procedure.

**Representative output**

3/4 can be rewritten as 6/8. Therefore:

3/4 = 6/8

6/8 > 5/8

So 3/4 > 5/8.

**Representation:** Use two equal wholes divided into eighths. Shade six eighths on one and five eighths on the other.

**Reasoning prompt:** How does rewriting 3/4 as 6/8 help you compare the fractions?

**Why useful:** The representation makes equivalence visible and connects the conceptual comparison to notation.

**Teacher check/customisation:** Ensure both wholes are identical and divided into equal parts. Do not use unequal visual regions.

## 4. Geometry and Measure

**Workflow:** GM-05 — Area

**Teacher input**

> Year 4. Create a short area sequence using rectangles, square units and reasoning. Include a distinction between area and perimeter.

**Representative output**

**Concept:** Area describes the amount of surface covered. It is measured in square units.

**Worked example:** A rectangle is 6 squares long and 4 squares wide. Its area is 6 × 4 = 24 square units.

**Reasoning question:** A rectangle has an area of 24 square units. Give two possible whole-number side lengths.

**Possible answers:** 1 × 24, 2 × 12, 3 × 8, 4 × 6.

**Misconception check:** A pupil says the perimeter is also 24. Ask: What does perimeter measure, and what unit would we use?

**Why useful:** The workflow connects the formula to covering and deliberately addresses area/perimeter confusion.

**Teacher check/customisation:** Use the year-group curriculum expectation and ensure the visual representation actually shows square units.

## 5. Problem Solving and Reasoning

**Workflow:** PR-04 — Always, sometimes, never

**Teacher input**

> Year 5. Create an always/sometimes/never task about multiples. Include justification and counterexamples.

**Representative output**

**Statement:** The sum of two multiples of 5 is a multiple of 5.

**Classification:** Always true.

**Justification:** If the numbers are 5a and 5b, their sum is 5a + 5b = 5(a+b), which is a multiple of 5.

**Statement:** The sum of two odd numbers is odd.

**Classification:** Never true.

**Counterexample:** 3 + 5 = 8. More generally, odd + odd is even.

**Why useful:** The workflow creates reasoning that requires pupils to justify a classification rather than guess from examples.

**Teacher check/customisation:** For younger pupils, use numerical examples and counterexamples before introducing symbolic generalisation.

## 6. Statistics and Data

**Workflow:** SD-03 — Bar charts and pictograms

**Teacher input**

> Year 4. Create a pictogram using a key of 2 children per symbol. Use four categories and include comparison questions.

**Representative output**

| Favourite fruit | Number of children |
|---|---:|
| Apples | 8 |
| Bananas | 6 |
| Oranges | 10 |
| Grapes | 4 |

**Key:** ● = 2 children.

**Questions:**
1. Which fruit was most popular?
2. How many more children chose oranges than grapes?
3. How many symbols are needed for apples?
4. How many children were represented altogether?

**Answers:** Oranges; 6; 4 symbols; 28 children.

**Why useful:** The workflow forces the key, source data and interpretation questions to remain consistent.

**Teacher check/customisation:** Check that every displayed symbol corresponds exactly to the stated key and that totals reconcile.

## 7. Assessment, Diagnosis and Intervention

**Workflow:** ADI-02 — Mathematics hinge question

**Teacher input**

> Year 5. Create a hinge question for decimal place value. The target misconception is believing that 0.35 is greater than 0.5 because 35 is greater than 5.

**Representative output**

**Hinge question:** Which is greater?

A. 0.35
B. 0.5
C. They are equal
D. There is not enough information

**Correct answer:** B.

**Diagnostic interpretation:**
- A may indicate comparison based on whole-number digit size rather than place value.
- C may indicate confusion about decimal equivalence.
- D may indicate uncertainty about comparing decimal quantities.

**Teacher response:** Ask pupils to represent both values using a hundred-square or equivalent place-value representation, then compare 35 hundredths with 50 hundredths.

**Why useful:** The distractors are designed to reveal different reasoning patterns rather than simply provide wrong answers.

**Teacher check/customisation:** Distractor interpretations should remain hypotheses until supported by additional evidence.
